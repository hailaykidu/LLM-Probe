\begin{abstract}
We evaluate eight general-purpose large language models on three
lexicon-grounded Tigrinya tasks --- part-of-speech tagging, morphosyntactic
labelling, and English$\rightarrow$Tigrinya translation --- using a 5,783-row
annotated bilingual lexicon. Our central methodological finding is that such
evaluations are easy to over-read, and that on these tasks the apparent
competence is an artefact of measurement rather than a property of the
models. A permissive matching rule puts the best model at 85.9\% on
part-of-speech tagging and 89.4\% on morphosyntactic labelling, but scoring
the same saved outputs by the first word the model commits to costs up to 26
points. More decisively, the prompt supplies an English gloss beside the
Tigrinya form, and an oracle that sees only that gloss scores 96.5\% and
95.9\% on the two tasks: \emph{no model comes within 14 points of what the
English alone makes available}, because fewer than one headword in twenty is
ambiguous given the English. On translation all eight models score at or
below chrF 0.81, while NLLB-200-3.3B, which supports Tigrinya, reaches chrF
16.05 on identical items with an identical scorer. We therefore report a
negative result: these tasks, as posed, do not measure Tigrinya competence,
and published numbers on them should not be read as doing so. We release the
lexicon, the scoring code and the saved model outputs, and argue that the
procedure applied here --- report every matching rule, bound the task from
above as well as below with references that use none of the target language,
and quantify prompt echo --- requires no resources beyond those an evaluation
already has, and should be standard in low-resource evaluation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Tigrinya is a Semitic language of Eritrea and northern Ethiopia with
approximately 9--10 million speakers and very little machine-readable
linguistic infrastructure. This scarcity makes it a natural test of whether
general-purpose LLMs generalise beyond high-resource languages, and a
difficult one to measure: without established benchmarks it is easy to
produce numbers that look like competence.

This paper makes three contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A lexicon-grounded benchmark} of 5,783 English--Tigrinya rows
    (4,331 English headwords, 3,767 Tigrinya forms), each carrying a
    part-of-speech label, derived from a digitised bilingual
    dictionary~\citep{swansea-dictionary}.
  \item \textbf{An evaluation of eight LLMs} across POS tagging,
    morphosyntactic labelling and translation, reported under three matching
    rules and against references that use no Tigrinya at all.
  \item \textbf{A negative result about the task design.} The choice of
    matching rule moves reported accuracy by up to 26 points on fixed
    outputs, and an oracle restricted to the English gloss scores 96.5\% and
    95.9\% on the two tagging tasks, which no model approaches. The tasks are
    therefore substantially solvable without the target language, and the
    numbers they produce --- including ours --- are not measurements of
    Tigrinya competence. We give the procedure that exposes this and argue it
    should be routine.
\end{enumerate}

The third contribution is the reason for this paper. An earlier version of
this work reported results computed with a permissive matching rule and no
baselines, and drew an architectural conclusion that does not survive either
correction. The authors have requested its retraction;
Section~\ref{sec:relation} states the relationship between the two.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Tigrinya and Ge'ez-script NLP}

Tigrinya remains severely underrepresented in NLP: a recent survey of over 50
studies from 2011 to 2025 finds a field whose progress is driven almost
entirely by discrete resource-creation milestones rather than by modelling
advances~\citep{gaim-park-2026-tigrinya}, a pattern echoed in broader surveys
of Ethiopian-language NLP~\citep{tonja-etal-2023-ethiopian}. The resources
that exist are small and task-specific. \citet{oktem-etal-2020-tigrinya} built
a domain-specific Tigrinya$\rightarrow$English NMT system using transfer
learning from other Ge'ez-script languages, reporting a 1.3 BLEU improvement
over a neural baseline; \citet{tela-etal-2020-transferring} showed that an
English XLNet fine-tuned on 10k examples reaches 78.88 F1 on Tigrinya
sentiment, outperforming mBERT by 7 points; TiGQA contributed 2,685
expert-annotated question--answer pairs from 537 context
paragraphs~\citep{teklehaymanot-etal-2024-tigqa}; and VEXMLM demonstrated that
vocabulary extension with 30k Ge'ez-script subwords substantially improves
XLM-R on Amharic and Tigrinya, attributing the baseline weakness to high
out-of-vocabulary rates under Latin-centric
tokenizers~\citep{teklehaymanot-etal-2026-vexmlm}. Tigrinya also appears as
one slice of multilingual benchmarks --- MasakhaPOS, the largest POS dataset
for African languages, covering 20 typologically diverse languages under UD
guidelines~\citep{dione-etal-2023-masakhapos}, and Belebele, a parallel
multiple-choice reading-comprehension set in 122 language
variants~\citep{bandarkar-etal-2024-belebele}. Our lexicon complements these:
it is not running text, and it tests the dictionary-headword level that sits
beneath all of them. The structural reason such resources are scarce at all is
the one \citet{joshi-etal-2020-state} documented --- representation in NLP
venues tracks resource availability, calling into question the ``language
agnostic'' status of current systems.

\subsection{LLM evaluation for low-resource languages}

NLLB-200 added Tigrinya (\texttt{tir\_Ethi}, Ge'ez script,
Afro-Asiatic/Semitic) to a 200-language translation
model~\citep{nllb-2022}, evaluated on FLORES, whose professionally
translated, multilingually aligned design set the standard for low-resource MT
benchmarking~\citep{goyal-etal-2022-flores}. Against that backdrop, several
studies have established that general-purpose LLMs lag purpose-built MT on
exactly the languages we study. \citet{robinson-etal-2023-chatgpt} evaluated
ChatGPT on 204 FLORES-200 varieties and found NLLB superior on 169 of 201
languages (84.1\%), by an average of 11.9 chrF, with the gap widest for
African languages and the Ge'ez script specifically; they further report that
five-shot prompting improved on zero-shot by only 0.88 chrF on average across
203 directions, which is consistent with our own few-shot probe moving
gemma-7b from chrF 0.16 to 0.66 with exact match unchanged.
\citet{hendy-etal-2023-gpt} reach the same conclusion for GPT models across
eighteen directions --- competitive for high-resource, limited for
low-resource --- and \citet{zhu-etal-2023-multilingual} find GPT-4 beating
NLLB in 40.91\% of directions overall while still trailing badly on the long
tail. \citet{adebara-etal-2025-where} extend this to a continent-scale African
benchmark and attribute the residual disparities to data inequities rather
than model scale. Our Section~\ref{subsec:translation} result --- three NLLB
checkpoints at chrF 14.1--16.1 against eight LLMs at $\leq$ 0.81 --- is this
finding at lexicon granularity, where the LLM floor is not merely lower but
absolute.

A separate strand asks whether LLM evaluations in these settings are conducted
carefully enough. LAraBench benchmarked 33 Arabic tasks across 61 datasets and
explicitly computed a random baseline ``to determine if the LLMs predictions
are not merely the result of chance''~\citep{abdelali-etal-2023-larabench}. We
regard that step as mandatory rather than optional, and
Section~\ref{sec:results} shows why.

\subsection{Trivial baselines and spurious cues}
\label{subsec:spurious}

The methodological core of this paper has a direct precedent in natural
language inference. \citet{poliak-etal-2018-hypothesis} showed that a
hypothesis-only model --- one that never sees the premise, a degenerate
solution by construction --- significantly outperforms a majority-class
baseline on a number of NLI datasets;
\citet{gururangan-etal-2018-annotation} showed the same artefacts let a simple
text classifier label the hypothesis alone correctly in about 67\% of SNLI and
53\% of MultiNLI, concluding that model success ``has been overestimated''.
\citet{mccoy-etal-2019-right} demonstrated that strong test-set scores can
rest on fallible syntactic heuristics that collapse on controlled sets, and
\citet{niven-kao-2019-probing} found BERT's 77\% on argument reasoning
comprehension to be entirely accounted for by spurious statistical cues. Our
English-only tagger is a hypothesis-only baseline in this sense: the prompt
format \texttt{Phrase: \{Tigrinya\} (\{English\})} leaves the part of speech
largely recoverable from the gloss, and the baseline reaching 60.38 is the
lexicon-task analogue of the 67\% SNLI result.

For POS tagging specifically, \citet{kann-etal-2020-weakly} make the closest
argument to ours: weakly supervised taggers reported to perform ``almost as
well as supervised ones'' were evaluated on languages unlike truly
low-resource ones, and on 15 genuinely low-resource languages the best model
gets fewer than half the words right, with a strongest baseline macro-average
of 39.11\%. They also note that such methods presuppose ``high-coverage and
almost error-free dictionaries'' --- precisely the assumption our
Section~\ref{subsec:iaa} annotator notes undermine, where the source
dictionary contains mislabelled parts of speech and outright mistranslations.
At the level of benchmark design, \citeauthor{reuel-etal-2024-betterbench}'s
assessment of 46 best practices across 24 benchmarks identifies stating random
performance as a scored criterion, since without it one cannot separate
capability from chance or metric design~\citep{reuel-etal-2024-betterbench}.

\subsection{Scoring rules, answer extraction and prompt artefacts}

Our finding that the matching rule moves reported accuracy by up to 26 points
on fixed outputs belongs to a growing literature on evaluation mechanics.
\citet{tam-etal-2024-speak} show that constraining LLMs to structured output
formats significantly degrades measured reasoning, and disentangle format
errors from content by using an LLM as a ``perfect parser'' rather than a
regex --- the mirror image of our problem, where permissive parsing
manufactures competence instead of destroying it.
\citet{kirouane-petrocheilos-2026-thinking} document the same failure mode
concretely in a low-resource setting: a scorer that scanned the whole response
instead of the requested answer line produced a $+29.8$ pp artefact,
one-directional against verbose outputs, and they conclude that ``any
benchmark that requests an answer format must score that format first and
report how often it was absent''. Our first-token rule is that recommendation
applied; our exact / first-token / set-intersection triple reports the spread
it would otherwise hide.

The prompt-echo column in Section~\ref{subsec:morph} relates to known
label-copying behaviour. \citet{zhao-etal-2021-calibrate} show that few-shot
LLM accuracy is destabilised by bias towards answers appearing in the prompt
or common in pretraining, varying ``from near chance to near
state-of-the-art'' with prompt format alone. When the gold distribution is
53.7\% \texttt{noun} and the prompt example contains the word \texttt{noun}, a
model that copies the example is scored correct by construction under
set-intersection --- gemma-7b does this on 97.4\% of answers. We therefore
treat echo rate as a reportable diagnostic rather than a footnote.

\subsection{Metrics and agreement statistics}

We report chrF~\citep{popovic-2015-chrf} because character $n$-gram F-score is
tokenisation-independent and therefore appropriate for Ge'ez-script targets
averaging 1.37 whitespace tokens, where word-level
BLEU~\citep{papineni-etal-2002-bleu} is near-undefined; we follow
\citeauthor{post-2018-call}'s argument that BLEU is a parameterised family
whose configurations are not comparable across papers unless the scheme is
pinned down~\citep{post-2018-call}, and report character-level BLEU alongside
chrF for this reason. Agreement is reported as Cohen's
$\kappa$~\citep{cohen-1960-coefficient}. We note the standard caveat that
$\kappa$ is hard to interpret under class imbalance --- the ``kappa paradox''
--- and that absolute thresholds are not meaningful without a contextual
reference~\citep{wong-etal-2021-cross}; this is why
Section~\ref{subsec:iaa} reports $\kappa$ beside raw observed agreement and
beside the filled-label-only variant, rather than reporting a single
coefficient.

\subsection{Dictionaries as evaluation resources}

Digitised bilingual dictionaries are a standard bottom-up resource for
languages lacking parallel corpora:
\citet{wickramasinghe-desilva-2023-sinhala} argue that for low-resource pairs
it is ``more feasible to move in the bottom-up direction where finer granular
pairs such as dictionary datasets are developed first'', and
\citeauthor{alnajjar-etal-2020-verdd}'s Ve'rdd addresses the complementary
problem of grassroots paper dictionaries edited by many hands, which require
re-evaluation rather than direct ingestion~\citep{alnajjar-etal-2020-verdd}.
Our source exhibits exactly the defects that motivate that work --- a
direction-swapped second block, inline gender markers embedded in the target
field, and annotator-flagged mistranslations --- and
Sections~\ref{subsec:source}--\ref{subsec:iaa} document them rather than
silently cleaning them.

\subsection{Relation to the earlier version}

An earlier version of this work introduced the lexicon and the four-task
framework used here. Section~\ref{sec:relation} states what it reported,
which of its claims survive re-scoring, and the authors' request for its
retraction.

\section{Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

\subsection{Source and construction}
\label{subsec:source}

The lexicon was digitised from the Swansea Tigrinya--English
dictionary~\citep{swansea-dictionary}, with additional entries contributed by
native-speaker linguists, and carries the source dictionary's own
part-of-speech abbreviations. The digitised source holds 7,234 rows
concatenated from two directions, English$\rightarrow$Tigrinya followed by
Tigrinya$\rightarrow$English, under a single pair of column headers; in the
second block the column named \texttt{English} therefore holds Ge'ez script.
We detect that block by script, reorient it, and merge both directions,
keeping distinct senses of one form as separate items --- \emph{estimate}
$\rightarrow$ \ti{ግምት} is retained as both noun and verb --- which yields
\textbf{5,783 gold rows} over 4,331 English headwords and 3,767 Tigrinya
forms. Three evaluation set sizes follow from this and are not
interchangeable: 5,783 gold rows, \textbf{5,775 items} for translation and
lexical alignment after each task drops duplicate pairs and rows with an
empty side, and \textbf{5,701 items} for the two tagging tasks, which were
run against a further reduction representing each multi-sense entry by its
first sense. Appendix~\ref{app:dataset} gives the row-level derivation, the
provenance of the source file, and the reconstruction of the 5,701-item set
from the released gold.

\subsection{What the annotations do and do not contain}
\label{subsec:annotations}

The POS labels are the source dictionary's, not independently re-annotated by
us. They are coarse: \texttt{noun} accounts for 3,109 of 5,783 labelled
entries (53.7\%), \texttt{verb} 1,542, \texttt{adjective} 879.

Morphosyntactic features are \textbf{sparse}. Only 185 entries carry gender,
number or plurality; the remaining 5,598 (96.8\%) carry a value identical to
the POS label. We therefore do not treat morphosyntactic labelling as a
distinct linguistic dimension, and report it in Section~\ref{subsec:morph} as
what it measures: a second POS task with a different prompt.

\subsection{Inter-annotator agreement}
\label{subsec:iaa}

% Camera-ready: replace "two of this paper's authors" with the authors'
% names if the venue expects them. The substance -- that the annotators are
% authors and that no third party adjudicated -- must stay either way.
A 500-item subset of the lexicon was annotated independently by two of this
paper's authors for part of speech, gender, number, category agreement and
lexical alignment. Each assigned labels separately using the annotation
guide, and the two sheets were then compared to locate disagreements. The
final sheet was compiled from those two sheets: \textbf{no third annotator
took part, and no party outside the author group adjudicated}. We state this
because it bounds what the coefficients below can mean, and because the
compiled sheet is sometimes read as an independent adjudication, which it is
not.

The records are released as
\texttt{POS\_english\_to\_tigrigna\_Annotated.xlsx} --- the two annotation
sheets, the compiled sheet and the annotation guide that defines each value
--- and \texttt{scripts/compute\_iaa.py} recomputes every figure below from
them.

An annotator sometimes left a cell empty where the other assigned a label.
Those are not matching judgements, so we count them as disagreements and
report Cohen's $\kappa$ over all 500 items, with a blank treated as its own
category (Table~\ref{tab:iaa}).

Restricting instead to the items both annotators labelled raises every
dimension --- 0.9076, 1.0000, 1.0000, 0.9815, 0.9850 on 500, 420, 428, 445 and
474 items. We report the stricter figures because the difference between the
two columns is largely a difference in what each annotator left blank:
Annotator 1 omits 32 gender, 32 number and 46 agreement labels that Annotator
2 supplies, against 6, 4 and 3 in the other direction. Discarding those rows
would hide the main source of divergence.

Three properties of this subset bound what the coefficients support.

\paragraph{The compiled sheet is not an independent third judgement.} This
follows from how it was produced, and the records bear it out: on the 25
part-of-speech items where the annotators differ, the compiled sheet
reproduces Annotator 2 on all 25 and Annotator 1 on none; on the three
gender, agreement and alignment disagreements it reproduces Annotator 1. It
selects between the two existing columns rather than adding a third
judgement, so agreement with it is not evidence about either annotator.

\paragraph{Agreement on filled labels is near-total.} Where both annotators
assigned a gender or number value, they never differ --- 0 disagreements
across 420 and 428 items. The divergence between them is entirely in coverage,
not in choice of label, which is why $\kappa$ falls as far as it does once
blanks count.

\paragraph{The subset is not a random sample.} Tracing each item to its source
sheet shows the 500 are the alphabetical head of each category: all 9
prepositions, all 10 pronouns and all 6 interjections, then 305 adjectives and
150 nouns from the tops of those sheets. The corpus is 54\% noun and 25\%
verb; this subset is 61\% adjective and contains \textbf{no verbs}. The
coefficients therefore describe agreement on this subset and are not an
estimate for the corpus. Establishing one requires a stratified
re-annotation covering verbs, which we leave to future work
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

The workbook also carries the annotators' free-text notes on 201 of the 500
items, documenting mislabelled parts of speech in the source dictionary,
inflected forms given as citation forms, and outright mistranslations
(\emph{at least} $\rightarrow$ \ti{ብብዚሒ}, which means \emph{at most}). These
support the caveat in Section~\ref{subsec:annotations} that the POS labels are
the source dictionary's and were not independently verified.

\paragraph{We do not compare architectures.} All five causal models are
instruction-tuned and all three sequence-to-sequence models are raw pretrained
checkpoints; the two factors are perfectly confounded in this model set and no
result here can separate them. The three base seq2seq models receive
span-infilling prompts matched to their pretraining objective; they
nonetheless produce almost no usable output, which we report as a property of
those checkpoints on this task rather than of their architecture.

Generation is greedy with \texttt{max\_new\_tokens=128},
\texttt{min\_new\_tokens=1}. \texttt{xlm-roberta-base}~\citep{conneau-etal-2020-unsupervised}
was included initially but fails on all tasks --- its prompts contain no
\texttt{<mask>} token --- and is excluded.

\subsection{Scoring}
\label{subsec:scoring}

Model answers are frequently verbose: gemma-7b's median POS answer is 14
words. We report three rules.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{exact} --- the cleaned answer equals the gold label.
  \item \textbf{first-token} --- the model's first word is a gold label word.
    This is the answer the model commits to, and is our headline rule.
  \item \textbf{set-intersection} --- any word of the answer is a gold label
    word. This is permissive: an answer naming several parts of speech is
    correct as soon as one fits. We report it only to show the gap.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{subsec:baselines}

The prompt supplies the English gloss alongside the Tigrinya form
(\texttt{Phrase: \{Tigrinya\} (\{English\})}), so a system can score well
without using the Tigrinya at all. We therefore bound the two tagging tasks
from below and from above with references that see no Tigrinya whatsoever.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Majority} --- always answer the most frequent gold label
    (\texttt{noun}).
  \item \textbf{English-only tagger} --- an NLTK~\citep{loper-bird-2002-nltk}
    English POS tagger applied to the gloss alone. This is what one
    off-the-shelf English system achieves, and is a lower bound on what the
    gloss makes available.
  \item \textbf{English-only ceiling} --- an oracle that sees only the
    English string and answers with the most frequent gold label that
    headword carries anywhere in the evaluation set. This is the best any
    English-only system can reach, and we report it because the lower bound
    alone understates how much of the task the gloss already determines.
\end{itemize}

The ceiling is high: 96.49 on part-of-speech tagging and 95.86 on
morphosyntactic labelling. Only 189 of 5,025 headwords (3.8\%) carry more
than one gold part-of-speech label, and 212 (4.2\%) more than one
morphosyntactic label, so the Tigrinya form can supply information the gloss
does not already give on fewer than one item in twenty. A model scoring below
the ceiling has not shown that it used the Tigrinya.

Differences against the tagger baseline are tested with a one-sided binomial
test at $n = 5{,}701$; \texttt{scripts/task\_ceiling.py} computes the ceiling,
the ambiguity counts and the tests.

Three models exceed the tagger baseline, each significantly
($p < 10^{-4}$): mistral-7b by 21.19 points, gemma-7b by 19.01 and gemma-2b
by 12.49. Falcon-7b and qwen-7b fall below the majority baseline; all three
seq2seq checkpoints are at or near zero.

\textbf{No model reaches the English-only ceiling.} The best, mistral-7b,
falls 14.93 points short of it; gemma-7b 17.10 and gemma-2b 23.63. Since the
ceiling is what a system achieves from the gloss alone, the ordering above is
not evidence that any model used the Tigrinya form. It is consistent with
every model using the gloss and using it worse than an English-only oracle
would.

The exact column shows how brittle these numbers are. Gemma-7b scores 0.00\%
exact and 79.39\% first-token: it always answers in a sentence, never a bare
label. A reader given only one of these columns would draw opposite
conclusions.

\subsection{Lexical alignment}
\label{subsec:alignment}

We report this task for completeness but do not draw conclusions from it:
2,208 of 5,783 alignment labels (38\%) are positional fallbacks
(\texttt{AlignmentSource: auto}) rather than annotation. Format accuracy
ranges from 0.0383 (mt5-large) to 0.5484 (gemma-2b); semantic accuracy from
0.0012 (mt5-small) to 0.8876 (mistral-7b). The two measures diverge widely for
individual models, which makes the task ill-posed as currently scored.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The matching rule is a reporting decision, not an implementation
detail.} On fixed model outputs, moving from set-intersection to first-token
costs gemma-7b 3.05 points on POS and 26.10 on morphosyntax; moving to exact
match costs it everything. A paper that reports one number without naming the
rule has not reported a result.

\paragraph{Bound the task from above, not only from below.} A lower-bound
baseline tells you whether a model beats a weak system; it does not tell you
whether the task needs the target language. On POS the majority answer scores
53.73 and an English tagger 60.38, and three models clear both --- which
reads as cross-lingual competence until the upper bound is added. An oracle
restricted to the English gloss reaches 96.49, and the best model is 14.93
points below it. The same holds on morphosyntax, where the gap is 31.28.
Reporting only the lower bounds would have let this paper claim a result it
does not have.

\paragraph{Prompt echo is a measurable confound.} Gemma-7b reproduces the
prompt's example in 97.4\% of its morphosyntax answers, and because the
example contains the majority gold label, a permissive scorer rewards it. We
recommend reporting echo rate wherever a prompt contains a worked example.

\paragraph{Purpose-built MT still dominates at the lexicon level.} NLLB-200
checkpoints reach chrF 14.11--16.05 where the best general-purpose model
reaches 0.81 --- more than an order of magnitude on chrF. For low-resource
languages, model selection dominates model size.

\paragraph{What we can claim about Tigrinya competence.} On the two tagging
tasks, nothing. Three models beat the English tagger on POS and two on
morphosyntax, each significantly at $n = 5{,}701$, but all eight sit well
below what the English gloss alone affords, and fewer than one headword in
twenty is ambiguous given that gloss. These tasks cannot separate a model
that reads Tigrinya from one that reads the English and answers worse than an
oracle would. Translation is the one task here that cannot be solved from the
prompt's English, and there the result is unambiguous: no model translates
into Tigrinya. Even NLLB, which supports the language, reaches only 16.24\%
exact on dictionary headwords, so the difficulty is real rather than an
artefact of our setup.

\section{Relation to an earlier version of this work}
\label{sec:relation}

An earlier version of this work, which introduced the lexicon and the
four-task framework used here, has been withdrawn at the authors' request.
Its part-of-speech and morphosyntactic results were computed with the
permissive matching rule described in Section~\ref{subsec:scoring}, under
which an answer counts as correct if any of its words matches a gold label
word, and were reported without baselines; it also drew a conclusion about
sequence-to-sequence architectures from a model set in which architecture and
instruction-tuning are confounded.

This paper re-scores the same saved model outputs under the three matching
rules of Section~\ref{subsec:scoring} and against the references of
Section~\ref{subsec:baselines}. Re-scoring alone changes the ordering and the
magnitudes, but the more consequential point is that it cannot rescue the
earlier claims: the two tagging tasks are bounded above at 96.49 and 95.86 by
a system that sees only the English gloss, and no model approaches either
bound. Those tasks could not have supported a claim about Tigrinya competence
under any matching rule. The morphosyntactic task is additionally not
distinct from part-of-speech tagging, its gold labels being 96.8\% identical,
and the architectural conclusion is confounded with instruction-tuning. This
is why the earlier version is withdrawn rather than corrected.

Every figure in this paper is computed from the released lexicon, the saved
generations and the scoring code listed in
Section~\ref{sec:reproducibility}. No number is carried over from the
withdrawn version, and the two sets of results should not be compared as
alternative measurements of the same quantity.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item POS labels are the source dictionary's, not independently
    re-annotated. The agreement coefficients in Section~\ref{subsec:iaa}
    describe a 500-item subset that is alphabetical and contains no verbs,
    annotated by two of this paper's authors rather than by annotators
    external to the work, and compiled without a third adjudicator. They are
    not an estimate for the corpus, and a stratified re-annotation covering
    verbs, by annotators outside the author group, would be needed for that.
  \item Morphosyntactic features cover 185 of 5,783 gold rows.
  \item Lexical alignment gold is 38\% positional fallback;
    Section~\ref{subsec:alignment} is not a result.
  \item Entries are dictionary headwords, not running text; performance here
    does not predict sentence-level performance.
  \item The model set cannot separate architecture from instruction-tuning. An
    instruction-tuned seq2seq model (\texttt{flan-t5}, \texttt{mt0}) would be
    needed.
  \item Both tagging tasks are substantially solvable without Tigrinya: an
    English-only oracle reaches 96.49 and 95.86, and under 5\% of headwords
    are ambiguous given the gloss. This is a property of the task design, not
    of the models, and it bounds what any result on these tasks can mean. A
    task that withheld the gloss, or that drew its items from the ambiguous
    subset, would measure something the English does not already determine;
    we have not run that experiment.
  \item The ceiling is computed from the evaluation set itself, so it is an
    oracle rather than a system: it knows the label distribution of each
    headword. It bounds what the English makes available on these items, not
    what an English-only system would achieve on unseen data.
\end{itemize}

\section{Dataset derivation}
\label{app:dataset}

This appendix records the row-level derivation summarised in
Section~\ref{subsec:source}. Every count is reproducible from the released
code: \texttt{build\_merged\_eval\_set.py --dry-run --verify} rebuilds the
gold files from the source CSV and compares them against what is released,
and \texttt{build\_sense\_split\_eval\_set.py --verify} does the same for the
5,701-item set.

\paragraph{Source and provenance.} The digitised file
\texttt{Combined\_POS\_Lexicon.csv} holds 7,234 rows: 3,587
English$\rightarrow$Tigrinya entries followed by 3,647
Tigrinya$\rightarrow$English entries. It is a flattening of the working
annotation workbook, whose eleven part-of-speech sheets hold the same 7,234
rows with the same per-category counts (3,929 noun, 1,784 verb, 993
adjective, 194 adverb, and so on). The flattening lifts the inline
gender and number markers that the workbook keeps inside the Tigrinya field
--- \texttt{(nf)} \ti{ወካሲት}, \ti{ከሳሲት} --- out into the label column, which
is the origin of the finer categories \texttt{(nm) noun masculine},
\texttt{(nf) noun feminine} and \texttt{(npl) noun plural}; the two files
differ on exactly the 247 rows carrying such a marker. The direction swap is
present in the workbook as well, in 3,635 of its 7,234 rows (50.2\%), so it
originates in digitisation rather than in the export.

\paragraph{7,234 counts rows, not independent annotations.} Of the 3,646
unique reverse-direction pairs, 1,448 (39.7\%) also occur in the forward
block.

\paragraph{From 7,234 to 5,783.} Merging the two directions deduplicates on
the (English, Tigrinya, features) triple rather than on the pair alone, so
that distinct senses of one form survive: \emph{estimate} $\rightarrow$
\ti{ግምት} is kept as both noun and verb, and \emph{grocer} $\rightarrow$
\ti{በዓል ድኳን} as both \texttt{noun} and \texttt{noun, masculine}. Expanding
comma-separated senses gives 5,864 atomic pairs.

\paragraph{From 5,783 to 5,775.} Each task script applies its own pair-level
deduplication when loading the gold, dropping 7 duplicate (English, Tigrinya)
pairs and 1 row with no English side.

\paragraph{From 5,775 to 5,701.} The two tagging tasks were run against a
gold file in which each multi-sense entry is represented by its first sense:
(\emph{a little}, \ti{ንእሽቶይ}, \ti{ቁሩብ}, \ti{ውሕድ}) was evaluated as
(\emph{a little}, \ti{ንእሽቶይ}). That intermediate file was not originally
released, so the task scripts as published would evaluate 5,775 items rather
than the 5,701 behind Sections~\ref{subsec:pos} and~\ref{subsec:morph}. We
reconstruct it from the released gold and verify the result row-for-row
against the saved model outputs: 5,701 items, no row missing, none extra, and
no label mismatch. The reconstructed file is released alongside the gold.
