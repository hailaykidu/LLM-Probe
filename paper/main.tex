\begin{abstract}
We evaluate eight general-purpose large language models on three
lexicon-grounded Tigrinya tasks (part-of-speech tagging, morphosyntactic
labelling, and English$\rightarrow$Tigrinya translation) using a 5,783-row
annotated bilingual lexicon. A fourth task, lexical alignment, is not well
enough posed to carry a result, and we release its data and outputs as a
resource for reposing it rather than as a benchmark.
Our central methodological finding is that such
evaluations are easy to over-read: on these tasks a high score is not
evidence of Tigrinya competence, because the tasks cannot distinguish
competence from much cheaper strategies. A permissive matching rule puts the
best model at 85.9\% on part-of-speech tagging and 89.4\% on morphosyntactic
labelling, but scoring the same saved outputs by the first word the model
commits to costs up to 26 points, and requiring an exact answer costs up to
89. More decisively, the prompt supplies an English gloss beside the
Tigrinya form, and an oracle that sees only that gloss scores 96.5\% and
95.9\% on the two tasks, which \emph{no model comes within 14 points of},
because only about one headword in twenty is ambiguous given the English.
What a model's score on these tasks therefore bounds is what the English
already determines, not what the model knows about Tigrinya. On translation all eight models score at or
below 0.81 chrF on a 0--100 scale, which is effectively no Tigrinya output at
all, while NLLB-200-3.3B, which supports the language, reaches 16.05 on
identical items with the same scorer: a factor of twenty. We therefore report a
negative result: these tasks, as posed, do not measure Tigrinya competence,
and published numbers on them should not be read as doing so. We release our
derived evaluation set, the scoring code and the saved model outputs, and
argue that the
procedure applied here (report every matching rule, bound the task from
above as well as below with references that use none of the target language,
and quantify prompt echo) requires no resources beyond those an evaluation
already has, and should be standard in low-resource evaluation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Tigrinya is a Semitic language of Eritrea and northern Ethiopia with
approximately 9--10 million speakers and very little machine-readable
linguistic infrastructure. This scarcity makes it a natural test of whether general-purpose LLMs generalise beyond high-resource languages, and a
difficult one to measure: without established benchmarks it is easy to
produce numbers that look like competence.

This paper makes three contributions.

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{A rebuilt and documented version of an existing
    lexicon-grounded evaluation set}: 5,783 English-Tigrinya rows (5,022
    distinct English headwords, 3,926 Tigrinya forms), each carrying a
    part-of-speech label, derived from a digitised bilingual
    dictionary~\citep{swansea-dictionary}. The lexicon and the four tasks
    are not new here; they were introduced by \citet{llmprobe-2026}, and
    Section~\ref{sec:relation} states what differs. What is new is that the
    set is reconstructible from its source by a released script, with its
    two digitisation defects corrected and its annotation provenance
    recorded.
  \item \textbf{An evaluation of eight LLMs} across POS tagging,
    morphosyntactic labelling and translation, reported under three matching
    rules and against references that use no Tigrinya at all. A fourth task
    in the benchmark, lexical alignment, is reported for completeness but not
    claimed as a result: 38\% of its gold labels are positional fallbacks
    rather than annotation (Section~\ref{subsec:alignment}).
  \item \textbf{A negative result about the task design.} The choice of
    matching rule moves reported accuracy on fixed outputs by up to 26 points
    between the two lenient rules and by as much as 89 points once exact
    match is included, and an oracle restricted to the English gloss scores
    96.5\% and 95.9\% on the two tagging tasks, which no model approaches.
    The tasks are therefore substantially solvable without the target
    language, and the numbers they produce, including ours, do not measure
    Tigrinya competence. We give the procedure that exposes this and argue it
    should be routine.
\end{enumerate}

This lexicon and these four tasks were introduced by
\citet{llmprobe-2026}, whose retraction has been requested. The results below come from new runs on a rebuilt gold set, not from re-scoring that paper's outputs; Section~\ref{sec:relation} gives the differences.

\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Tigrinya and Ge'ez-script NLP}

Tigrinya remains severely underrepresented in NLP: a survey of over 50
studies from 2011 to 2025 finds a field driven almost entirely by discrete
resource-creation milestones rather than modelling
advances~\citep{gaim-park-2026-tigrinya}, a pattern echoed across Ethiopian
languages~\citep{tonja-etal-2023-ethiopian}. Existing resources are small
and task-specific: domain-adapted
Tigrinya$\rightarrow$English NMT~\citep{oktem-etal-2020-tigrinya}, sentiment
transfer from English XLNet~\citep{tela-etal-2020-transferring}, two
question-answering sets~\citep{gaim-etal-2023-tiquad,teklehaymanot-etal-2024-tigqa},
and neural taggers over the small Nagaoka corpus, where a BiLSTM reaches
92\%~\citep{tesfagergish-kapociute-2020-pos}. Those taggers are trained on
running text; we evaluate untrained models on dictionary headwords.

Tigrinya appears in some multilingual benchmarks, such as
Belebele~\citep{bandarkar-etal-2024-belebele}, and is absent from others:
MasakhaPOS, the largest POS dataset for African languages, covers 20
languages under UD guidelines but not
Tigrinya~\citep{dione-etal-2023-masakhapos}. AfroBench reports the same gap
over 64 languages and 15 tasks, with LLM performance tracking monolingual
data availability~\citep{ojo-etal-2025-afrobench} --- the pattern
\citet{joshi-etal-2020-state} documented, where representation in NLP venues
tracks resource availability. This also bears on how a positive result
should be read, since \citet{blevins-zettlemoyer-2022-language} show English
pretraining corpora carry enough incidental non-English text to explain much
cross-lingual transfer, so apparent Tigrinya ability need not reflect
deliberate coverage.

The lexicon and the four tasks used here were introduced by
\citet{llmprobe-2026}, whose retraction has been requested;
Section~\ref{sec:relation} states what this paper takes from that work and
how the two differ.

\subsection{LLM evaluation for low-resource languages}

NLLB-200 added Tigrinya (\texttt{tir\_Ethi}) to a 200-language translation
model~\citep{nllb-2022}, evaluated on FLORES, whose professionally
translated design set the standard for low-resource MT
benchmarking~\citep{goyal-etal-2022-flores}. Several studies establish that
general-purpose LLMs lag purpose-built MT on exactly these languages.
\citet{robinson-etal-2023-chatgpt} found NLLB superior to ChatGPT on 169 of
201 FLORES-200 languages (84.1\%) by an average of 11.9 chrF, the gap widest
for African languages and the Ge'ez script, and report five-shot prompting
improving on zero-shot by only 0.88 chrF across 203 directions --- close to
our own few-shot probe, which moves gemma-7b from chrF 0.16 to 0.66 with
exact match unchanged. \citet{hendy-etal-2023-gpt} reach the same conclusion
for GPT models across eighteen directions, competitive for high-resource and
limited for low-resource, and \citet{zhu-etal-2023-multilingual} find GPT-4
beating NLLB in 40.91\% of directions while trailing badly on the long tail.
\citet{adebara-etal-2025-where} extend this to a continent-scale African
benchmark and attribute the residual disparities to data inequities rather
than model scale. Our Section~\ref{subsec:translation} result --- three NLLB
checkpoints at chrF 14.1--16.1 against eight LLMs at $\leq$ 0.81 --- is this
finding at lexicon granularity, where the LLM floor is not merely lower but
absolute.

A separate strand asks whether such evaluations are conducted carefully
enough. LAraBench benchmarked 33 Arabic tasks across 61 datasets and
explicitly computed a random baseline ``to determine if the LLMs predictions
are not merely the result of chance''~\citep{abdelali-etal-2023-larabench}.
We regard that step as mandatory rather than optional, and
Section~\ref{sec:results} shows why.

\subsection{Trivial baselines and spurious cues}
\label{subsec:spurious}

The methodological core of this paper has a direct precedent in natural
language inference. \citet{poliak-etal-2018-hypothesis} showed that a
hypothesis-only model --- one that never sees the premise, a degenerate
solution by construction --- significantly outperforms a majority-class
baseline on several NLI datasets, and
\citet{gururangan-etal-2018-annotation} showed the same artefacts let a
classifier label the hypothesis alone correctly in about 67\% of SNLI,
concluding that model success ``has been overestimated''. Related work finds
strong scores resting on fallible syntactic
heuristics~\citep{mccoy-etal-2019-right} or on spurious statistical cues
that account for a model's performance
entirely~\citep{niven-kao-2019-probing}. Our English-only tagger is a
hypothesis-only baseline in this sense: the prompt format \texttt{Phrase:
\{Tigrinya\} (\{English\})} leaves the part of speech largely recoverable
from the gloss, and the baseline reaching 60.38 is the lexicon-task analogue
of that 67\% SNLI result.

For POS tagging specifically, \citet{kann-etal-2020-weakly} make the closest
argument to ours: weakly supervised taggers reported to perform ``almost as
well as supervised ones'' were evaluated on languages unlike truly
low-resource ones, and on 15 genuinely low-resource languages the best model
gets fewer than half the words right. They also note that such methods
presuppose ``high-coverage and almost error-free dictionaries'' --- precisely
the assumption the annotator notes behind Section~\ref{subsec:iaa} undermine,
where the source dictionary carries mislabelled parts of speech and outright
mistranslations. At the level of benchmark design,
\citet{reuel-etal-2024-betterbench} assess 46 best practices across 24
benchmarks and score stating random performance as a criterion, since
without it capability cannot be separated from chance or metric design.

\subsection{Scoring rules and prompt artefacts}

Our finding that the matching rule moves reported accuracy on fixed outputs
by up to 26 points between lenient rules, and by as much as 89 points once
exact match is included, belongs to a growing literature on evaluation
mechanics. \citet{tam-etal-2024-speak} show that constraining LLMs to
structured output formats degrades measured reasoning, and separate format
errors from content by using an LLM as a ``perfect parser'' rather than a
regex --- the mirror image of our problem, where permissive parsing
manufactures competence instead of destroying it.
\citet{kirouane-petrocheilos-2026-thinking} document the same failure mode
in a low-resource setting: a scorer that scanned the whole response instead
of the requested answer line produced a $+29.8$ pp artefact, one-directional
against verbose outputs, concluding that ``any benchmark that requests an
answer format must score that format first and report how often it was
absent''. Our first-token rule is that recommendation applied, and the
three-rule triple reports the spread it would otherwise hide.

The prompt-echo column in Section~\ref{subsec:morph} relates to known
label-copying behaviour: \citet{zhao-etal-2021-calibrate} show few-shot
accuracy destabilised by bias towards answers appearing in the prompt,
varying ``from near chance to near state-of-the-art'' with prompt format
alone. When the gold distribution is 53.8\% \texttt{noun} and the prompt
example contains \texttt{noun}, a model that copies the example is correct
by construction under set-intersection --- gemma-7b does this on 97.4\% of
answers --- so we treat echo rate as a reportable diagnostic.

\subsection{Metrics, agreement and dictionary sources}

We report chrF~\citep{popovic-2015-chrf} because character $n$-gram F-score
is tokenisation-independent and so appropriate for Ge'ez-script targets
averaging 1.18 whitespace tokens, where word-level
BLEU~\citep{papineni-etal-2002-bleu} is near-undefined; following
\citet{post-2018-call}, who argues that BLEU is a parameterised family whose
configurations are not comparable across papers unless the scheme is pinned
down, we report character-level BLEU alongside it. Agreement is Cohen's
$\kappa$~\citep{cohen-1960-coefficient}, reported beside raw observed
agreement and the filled-label-only variant rather than as a single
coefficient, because $\kappa$ is hard to interpret under class imbalance ---
the ``kappa paradox'' --- and absolute thresholds are not meaningful without
a contextual reference~\citep{wong-etal-2021-cross}.

Digitised bilingual dictionaries are a standard bottom-up resource for
languages lacking parallel corpora:
\citet{wickramasinghe-desilva-2023-sinhala} argue that for low-resource
pairs it is ``more feasible to move in the bottom-up direction where finer
granular pairs such as dictionary datasets are developed first'', while
Ve'rdd addresses the complementary problem of grassroots paper dictionaries
edited by many hands, which need re-evaluation rather than direct
ingestion~\citep{alnajjar-etal-2020-verdd}. Our source shows exactly the
defects that motivate that work --- a direction-swapped second block, inline
gender markers inside the target field, and annotator-flagged
mistranslations --- which Sections~\ref{subsec:source} to~\ref{subsec:iaa}
document rather than silently clean.

\section{Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

\subsection{Source and construction}
\label{subsec:source}

The lexicon was digitised from the Swansea Tigrinya--English
dictionary~\citep{swansea-dictionary}, with additional entries contributed by
native-speaker linguists, and carries the source dictionary's own
part-of-speech abbreviations. The digitised source holds 7,234 rows
concatenated from two directions, English$\rightarrow$Tigrinya followed by
Tigrinya$\rightarrow$English, under a single pair of column headers; in the
second block the column named \texttt{English} therefore holds Ge'ez script.
We detect that block by script, reorient it, and merge both directions,
keeping distinct senses of one form as separate items --- \emph{estimate}
$\rightarrow$ \ti{ግምት} is retained as both noun and verb, which yields
\textbf{5,783 gold rows} over 5,022 distinct English headwords and 3,926
distinct Tigrinya forms. Counts of headwords are sensitive to the
definition: splitting the comma-separated variants that some entries carry
in the English field gives 4,327 single-word glosses instead. We count whole
field values throughout, which is what the baselines and the ceiling in
Section~\ref{subsec:baselines} key on. Three evaluation set sizes follow
from this and are not
interchangeable: 5,783 gold rows, \textbf{5,775 items} for translation and
lexical alignment after each task drops duplicate pairs and rows with an
empty side, and \textbf{5,701 items} for the two tagging tasks, which were
run against a further reduction representing each multi-sense entry by its
first sense. Appendix~\ref{app:dataset} gives the row-level derivation, the
provenance of the source file, and the reconstruction of the 5,701-item set
from the released gold.

\subsection{What the annotations do and do not contain}
\label{subsec:annotations}

The POS labels are the source dictionary's, not independently re-annotated by
us. They are coarse: \texttt{noun} accounts for 3,109 of 5,783 labelled
entries (53.8\%), \texttt{verb} 1,542, \texttt{adjective} 879.

Morphosyntactic features are \textbf{sparse}. Only 183 entries carry a
morphosyntactic label that differs from the POS label at all; the remaining
5,600 (96.8\%) are identical to it. The 183 are sparser still than that
suggests: 88 carry gender, number or plurality (38 \texttt{noun,
masculine}, 35 \texttt{noun, feminine}, 13 \texttt{noun, plural}, 2
\texttt{uncategorized}), and the other 95 are \texttt{adjective,
noun-derived}, a derivational note rather than an inflectional feature. We therefore do not treat morphosyntactic labelling as a
distinct linguistic dimension, and report it in Section~\ref{subsec:morph} as
what it measures: a second POS task with a different prompt.

\subsection{Inter-annotator agreement}
\label{subsec:iaa}

% Wording here is anonymous and must stay so under the review option; see
% CAMERA_READY.md for what changes once the byline is in place.
A 500-item subset was annotated independently by two of the authors, both
native speakers of Tigrinya, for part of speech, gender, number, category
agreement and lexical alignment. Each assigned labels separately using the
annotation guide; the two sheets were then compared and a final sheet
compiled from them. \textbf{No third annotator took part, and no party
outside the author group adjudicated}, which bounds what the coefficients
below can mean: the compiled sheet is sometimes read as an independent
adjudication, and it is not one. The records --- both annotation sheets, the
compiled sheet and the guide that defines every permitted value --- are
released as a single workbook, and \texttt{scripts/compute\_iaa.py}
recomputes every figure here from it. The
workbook carries no edit history: the annotators exchanged sheets as
messaging attachments that have since expired, so the labels survive but the
order in which they were entered does not.

The five dimensions are not of one kind. The guide's instruction is to
``verify and annotate'': four are assigned from scratch, while the
part-of-speech label arrives pre-filled from the source dictionary and is
verified. The annotators used that latitude very differently --- Annotator 1
corrected the dictionary's part of speech on 25 of 500 items, Annotator 2 on
none --- so all 25 part-of-speech disagreements have one shape: one
annotator exercising the verification the guide asks for, the other
accepting the dictionary. Where an annotator left a cell empty and the other
assigned a label, that is not a matching judgement, so we count it as a
disagreement and report Cohen's $\kappa$ over all 500 items with a blank as
its own category (Table~\ref{tab:iaa}, in Appendix~\ref{app:iaa}).


Restricting instead to the items both annotators labelled raises every
dimension --- 0.9076, 1.0000, 1.0000, 0.9815 and 0.9850 on 500, 420, 428, 445
and 474 items. We report the stricter figures because the difference between the
two columns is largely a difference in what each annotator left blank:
Annotator 1 omits 32 gender, 32 number and 46 agreement labels that Annotator
2 supplies, against 6, 4 and 3 in the other direction. Discarding those rows
would hide the main source of divergence.

Three properties bound what these coefficients support, and
Appendix~\ref{app:iaa} gives the evidence for each. The compiled sheet is a
selection between the two existing columns, not an independent third
judgement, so it cannot serve as an adjudicated gold standard. Agreement on
filled labels is near-total: where both annotators assigned a gender or
number value they never differ, so the divergence is in coverage rather than
in choice of label, which is why $\kappa$ falls once blanks count. And the
subset is not a random sample but the alphabetical head of each category:
61\% adjective against a corpus that is 54\% noun and 25\% verb, and
\textbf{drawn from no verb entries at all}. The coefficients therefore
describe this subset and are not an estimate for the corpus; establishing one
needs a stratified re-annotation covering verbs, by annotators outside the
author group, which we leave to future work (Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

The workbook also carries the annotators' free-text notes on 201 of the 500
items, documenting mislabelled parts of speech in the source dictionary,
inflected forms given as citation forms, and outright mistranslations
(\emph{at least} $\rightarrow$ \ti{ብብዚሒ}, which means \emph{at most}). These
support the caveat in Section~\ref{subsec:annotations} that the POS labels are
the source dictionary's and were not independently verified.

\paragraph{A later run on newer models.} The eight models above are those
the evaluation was run on in full. They are dated: none exceeds 7B
parameters and three cannot follow an instruction. A later run on nine newer
and larger checkpoints reached its time limit after part-of-speech tagging
alone, so it is not in the tables; its log and generations are released, and
its best model scores above ours, Falcon3-10B-Instruct reaching 82.62 under
the task script's own scorer against mistral-7b's 78.76.

That illustrates this paper's argument rather than undercutting it.
Falcon3-10B emits its chat template into all 5,701 answers
(\texttt{<|assistant|> adverb}), so under the first-token rule it scores
\textbf{0.00}; strip the prefix and the same outputs give 80.25; score by
set intersection and they give 82.76. One model, one set of generations, a
range of 82 points. A larger model does not escape the measurement problem,
and 82.76 is still below the 96.49 an English-only oracle reaches, so the
higher score is not evidence that it read the Tigrinya.

\paragraph{We do not compare architectures.} All five causal models are
instruction-tuned and all three sequence-to-sequence models are raw pretrained
checkpoints; the two factors are perfectly confounded in this model set and no
result here can separate them. The three base seq2seq models receive
span-infilling prompts matched to their pretraining objective; they
nonetheless produce almost no usable output, which we report as a property of
those checkpoints on this task rather than of their architecture.

Generation sets \texttt{max\_new\_tokens=128} and
\texttt{min\_new\_tokens=1} and nothing else, so each checkpoint decodes
under its own \texttt{generation\_config.json}. This is not uniform:
Qwen1.5-7B-Chat ships \texttt{do\_sample=true} at temperature 0.7, so
qwen-7b was sampled, not decoded greedily. We also did not apply the
instruction-tuned models' chat templates; prompts were passed as plain text,
which is one reason the off-format answers of Section~\ref{subsec:scoring}
are so common. Both are limitations rather than design choices, and neither
affects claims about what the tasks can measure on fixed outputs.
\texttt{xlm-roberta-base}~\citep{conneau-etal-2020-unsupervised}
was included initially but fails on all tasks (its prompts contain no
\texttt{<mask>} token) and is excluded.

\subsection{Scoring}
\label{subsec:scoring}

Model answers are frequently verbose: gemma-7b's median POS answer is 14
words. We report three rules.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{exact}: the cleaned answer equals the gold label.
  \item \textbf{first-token}: the model's first word is a gold label word.
    This is the answer the model commits to, and is our headline rule.
  \item \textbf{set-intersection}: any word of the answer is a gold label
    word. This is permissive: an answer naming several parts of speech is
    correct as soon as one fits. We report it only to show the gap.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Baselines}
\label{subsec:baselines}

The prompt supplies the English gloss alongside the Tigrinya form
(\texttt{Phrase: \{Tigrinya\} (\{English\})}), so a system can score well
without using the Tigrinya at all. We therefore bound the two tagging tasks
from below and from above with references that see no Tigrinya whatsoever.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Majority}: always answer the most frequent gold label
    (\texttt{noun}).
  \item \textbf{English-only tagger}: an NLTK~\citep{loper-bird-2002-nltk}
    English POS tagger applied to the gloss alone. This is what one
    off-the-shelf English system achieves, and is a lower bound on what the
    gloss makes available.
  \item \textbf{English-only ceiling}: an oracle that sees only the
    English string and answers with the most frequent gold label that
    headword carries anywhere in the evaluation set. This is the best any
    English-only system can reach, and we report it because the lower bound
    alone understates how much of the task the gloss already determines.
\end{itemize}

The ceiling is high: 96.49 on part-of-speech tagging and 95.86 on
morphosyntactic labelling. Only 189 of 5,025 headwords (3.8\%) carry more
than one gold part-of-speech label, and 212 (4.2\%) more than one
morphosyntactic label. Those headwords account for 439 of the 5,701 items
(7.7\%) on part of speech and 499 (8.8\%) on morphosyntax, and they are the
only items on which the Tigrinya form can supply information the gloss does
not already determine. A model scoring below the ceiling has not shown that
it used the Tigrinya.

Differences against the tagger baseline are tested with a one-sided McNemar
test at $n = 5{,}701$. The test must be paired: the baseline is a system
scored on the same items as the model, so the two sets of outcomes are
correlated and only the discordant items carry information. Treating the
baseline rate as a known constant and applying a binomial test, as is
sometimes done, understates the variance of the difference and so overstates
significance; on our data it moves the smallest margin by an order of
magnitude ($p = 0.003$ against $p = 0.032$ for gemma-7b on morphosyntax).
\texttt{scripts/task\_ceiling.py} computes the ceiling, the ambiguity counts
and both tests.

All eight general-purpose models are at the floor. NLLB-200, for which

best LLM.

The comparison is not like-for-like, and the differences all favour NLLB.
It is a dedicated translation model given a source sentence and a target
language code, decoded with five-beam search; the LLMs are prompted in
English, decode under their own defaults (Section~\ref{subsec:scoring}), and
have their answers scored leniently, any word of a verbose reply counting as
a hit against any gold synonym. We draw only the conclusion this supports:
a purpose-built model that covers the language produces recognisable
Tigrinya on these items and the general-purpose models produce almost none,
a gap of a factor of twenty that no plausible decoding or prompting
advantage would close. It does not license a ranking of NLLB against these
LLMs as systems.

Note that word-level BLEU-4 is 0.24 for NLLB-3.3B, which translates 16.24\% of
items exactly. \textbf{Word-level BLEU is not usable at lexicon scale}, and a
paper reporting it would show a working system as scoring zero.

Our own scorer supplied a further instance of the effect this paper reports.
Many gold entries end in a trailing comma, and splitting them on commas
without discarding the empty final field admitted the empty string as a valid
answer, so a model that generated nothing scored an exact match. This gave
qwen-7b an exact-match rate of 0.28, the highest of the eight models and all
16 of its apparent successes empty generations. The released scorer drops
empty candidates and the table reports 0.00; no other model was affected and
no chrF or BLEU value changes. We report the defect rather than silently
correcting it because a one-line omission in a scorer, not any property of a
model, produced the best exact-match score in the table.

We verified the LLM failure is not one of output format. On a 300-item
random sample (seed 0, exemplar phrases excluded), with gemma-7b alone,
few-shot prompting with eight exemplars
and an explicit format constraint moved chrF from 0.16 to 0.66, and exact
match stayed at 0.00 on every arm of that probe; extracting the Ge'ez span
from verbose answers recovered the reference in 2 of the 5,775 full-set
items. (Gemma-7b's 0.03 in Table~\ref{tab:translation} is the full set,
where 2 items match; the probe's 0.00 is the 300-item sample, where none
does.) The
median character-level similarity between model output and reference is 0.000:
the models emit Ge'ez-shaped strings that are not the target words
(\ti{ባህል} for \ti{ብዙሕ}, \ti{ልልል} as repetition).

\subsection{Lexical alignment}
\label{subsec:alignment}

We report this task for completeness but draw no conclusion from it: 2,208
of 5,783 alignment labels (38\%) are positional fallbacks
(\texttt{AlignmentSource: auto}) rather than annotation, and its two
measures diverge widely per model --- format accuracy from 0.0383
(mt5-large) to 0.5484 (gemma-2b), semantic accuracy from 0.0012 (mt5-small)
to 0.8876 (mistral-7b) --- which is the paper's point again, two defensible
scorers disagreeing on fixed outputs by more than the models differ. We
release its data and outputs as a resource for reposing the task: the 3,575
verified rows are marked, so restricting to them or annotating the other
2,208 would give a task this one cannot.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{The matching rule is a reporting decision, not an implementation
detail.} The swings in Section~\ref{subsec:pos} are not a property of the
weaker models: the largest belong to the models that answer in fluent
sentences, because a sentence contains the gold label without equalling it.
Scoring the same saved outputs three ways costs gemma-7b everything it
scored. A paper that reports one number without naming the rule has not
reported a result, and reporting the triple costs nothing once the
generations are saved.

\paragraph{Bound the task from above, not only from below.} A lower-bound
baseline tells you whether a model beats a weak system; it does not tell you
whether the task needs the target language. Three models clear both lower
baselines on POS, which reads as cross-lingual competence until the oracle is
added and the best of them sits 14.93 points beneath it (31.28 on
morphosyntax). Reporting only the lower bounds would have let this paper
claim a result it does not have. The oracle costs one pass over the gold
labels, and we recommend it wherever a prompt carries a cue in a
higher-resource language.

\paragraph{Prompt echo is a measurable confound.} Gemma-7b reproduces the
prompt's example in 97.4\% of its morphosyntax answers, and because the
example contains the majority gold label, a permissive scorer rewards it.
Echo rate belongs in any report whose prompt contains a worked example.

\paragraph{What we can claim about Tigrinya competence.} On the two tagging
tasks, nothing: they cannot separate a model that reads Tigrinya from one
that reads the English gloss and answers worse than an oracle would.
Translation is the one task here not solvable from the prompt's English, and
there the result is unambiguous --- no model translates into Tigrinya, while
NLLB-200, which supports the language, is more than an order of magnitude
better on chrF. Model selection dominates model size; and even NLLB reaches
only 16.24\% exact on dictionary headwords, so the difficulty is real rather
than an artefact of our setup.

\section{Relation to prior work, and reproducibility}
\label{sec:relation}
\label{sec:reproducibility}

This lexicon and these four tasks were introduced by \citet{llmprobe-2026},
whose retraction has been requested. The results here are not a re-scoring
of that paper's outputs: they come from new runs in September 2026 against a
gold set rebuilt from the source lexicon, and differ in inputs as well as
scoring. The earlier runs evaluated 3,899 items per tagging task against
these 5,701, and the earlier gold stored an expected tag as
\texttt{(adv)~adverb} rather than \texttt{adverb} and presented all
comma-separated senses rather than one, so the prompts and the generations
they elicit both differ. No number is carried over, and the two sets of
results are not alternative measurements of the same quantity.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\begin{itemize}
  \item POS labels are the source dictionary's, verified but not
    independently re-annotated. The agreement coefficients in
    Section~\ref{subsec:iaa} describe a 500-item subset that is alphabetical
    and drawn from no verb entries, annotated by two of the authors rather
    than by annotators external to the work, and compiled without a third
    adjudicator. They are not an estimate for the corpus, and a
    stratified re-annotation covering verbs, by annotators outside the author
    group, would be needed for that.
  \item No edit history survives for the annotation. The annotators exchanged
    their sheets as messaging attachments, and those attachments have since
    expired, so the files that would carry a version history are no longer
    retrievable. The labels are intact in the released workbook; what cannot
    be evidenced is the order and timing in which they were entered.
  \item Morphosyntactic labels differ from the POS label on 183 of 5,783
    gold rows, of which 88 carry gender, number or plurality.
  \item Lexical alignment gold is 38\% positional fallback;
    Section~\ref{subsec:alignment} is not a result.
  \item Entries are dictionary headwords, not running text; performance here
    does not predict sentence-level performance.
  \item The model set cannot separate architecture from instruction-tuning. An
    instruction-tuned seq2seq model (\texttt{flan-t5}, \texttt{mt0}) would be
    needed. It is also dated: none of the eight exceeds 7B parameters, and
    three are span-denoising checkpoints that cannot follow an instruction at
    all. A later partial run on newer and larger models
    (Section~\ref{subsec:scoring}) scores above our best model on POS, which
    we report rather than leave in the logs, though the ceiling result means
    a higher score on this task is not evidence of Tigrinya competence.
  \item Decoding was each checkpoint's own default rather than a uniform
    greedy setting, so qwen-7b was sampled at temperature 0.7, and no chat
    template was applied to the instruction-tuned models. Single run, no
    seed averaging.
  \item Both tagging tasks are substantially solvable without Tigrinya: an
    English-only oracle reaches 96.49 and 95.86, and under 5\% of headwords
    are ambiguous given the gloss. This is a property of the task design, not
    of the models, and it bounds what any result on these tasks can mean. A
    task that withheld the gloss, or that drew its items from the ambiguous
    subset, would measure something the English does not already determine;
    we have not run that experiment.
  \item The ceiling is computed from the evaluation set itself, so it is an
    oracle rather than a system: it knows the label distribution of each
    headword. It bounds what the English makes available on these items, not
    what an English-only system would achieve on unseen data.
\end{itemize}

\section*{Ethics statement}

The annotators are two of this paper's authors, both native speakers of
Tigrinya, who annotated as part of their own research and received no
separate compensation; no external or crowd workers were engaged, and no
personal data was collected. The source material is a published bilingual
dictionary and the evaluation items are dictionary headwords, which carry no
information about individuals.

Each model was run once, with no seed averaging, under the decoding settings
of Section~\ref{subsec:scoring}. Evaluation used one node with four NVIDIA
A100 80GB GPUs. The reported results come from two jobs: 66.3 hours wall
clock for part-of-speech tagging, morphosyntax and lexical alignment over
the eight models, and 13.9 hours for the translation rerun, about 321
GPU-hours together. The partial run on the newer roster
(Section~\ref{subsec:scoring}) consumed a further 288 GPU-hours before
reaching its time limit. Every number in this paper is then recomputed from
the saved outputs on CPU in under a minute, which is the point of releasing
the generations.

chrF and character BLEU are computed with NLTK rather than sacreBLEU, so our
chrF values are not directly comparable to sacreBLEU figures reported
elsewhere; every comparison we draw is within this paper, between outputs
scored by the same implementation, including the NLLB comparison. Package
versions are listed in the released requirements file. The lexicon, gold files, saved
outputs, annotation workbook and scoring code accompany this submission and
will be released publicly on acceptance; Appendix~\ref{app:repro} gives the
command that recomputes each reported table from them.

A risk specific to this work: a benchmark that reports high accuracy on a
low-resource language can be cited as evidence that the language is served
when it is not, which may divert effort from building the resources it
actually needs. That is the failure this paper documents in its own earlier
numbers, and it is why we report the task ceiling alongside every score.

What the released workbook does not carry is the history of how it was
filled. The annotators exchanged their sheets as messaging attachments, and
those attachments have since expired, so the files that would carry an edit
history are no longer retrievable. The labels themselves are intact and are
what the workbook contains; what is missing is the order and timing in which
they were entered. We note this because the guide was applied unevenly in
ways the figures below expose, and a record of the process would have shown
whether that reflects a change of convention partway through.

The five dimensions are not of one kind, and the guide says so. Its
instruction is to ``verify and annotate'' the dictionary entries: gender,
number, category agreement and lexical alignment are assigned from scratch
against the definitions the guide gives, while the part-of-speech label
arrives pre-filled from the source dictionary and is verified. The guide
treats it as given material elsewhere too, defining category agreement as
whether the Tigrinya entry matches ``the English item and its POS label''.

The two annotators used that latitude very differently. Annotator 1 corrected
the dictionary's part of speech on 25 of 500 items; Annotator 2 corrected it
on none, letting the source label stand everywhere. All 25 part-of-speech
disagreements are therefore of one shape: one annotator exercising the
verification the guide asks for, and the other accepting the dictionary.

Three properties of this subset bound what the coefficients support.

\paragraph{The compiled sheet is not an independent third judgement.} This
follows from how it was produced, and the records bear it out. The two
annotators differ on 28 cells in total: 25 part of speech, one category
agreement and two lexical alignment. On the 25 part-of-speech items the
compiled sheet reproduces Annotator 2 on all 25 and Annotator 1 on none; on
the other three it reproduces Annotator 1. \textbf{On none of the 28 does it
record a label that neither annotator assigned.} Since Annotator 2's
part-of-speech column is the source dictionary's throughout, the compilation
rejected all 25 of Annotator 1's proposed corrections and deferred to the
dictionary in every case. It selects between the two
existing columns rather than adding a judgement of its own, so agreement with
it is not evidence about either annotator, and it cannot serve as an
adjudicated gold standard.

A third annotator would not repair this. The coefficients in
Table~\ref{tab:iaa} are computed between the two annotation columns and would
be unchanged by a third. The divergence between those columns is in any case
not a matter of conflicting labels: on gender and number the annotators never
differ on an item both labelled, and what separates them is how often each
left a cell empty. What the subset would need is a fresh annotation,
stratified over the categories and including verbs, by annotators outside the
author group; we report that as future work in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations} rather than adjudicating the present records
after the fact.

\paragraph{Agreement on filled labels is near-total.} Where both annotators
assigned a gender or number value, they never differ --- 0 disagreements
across 420 and 428 items. The divergence between them is entirely in coverage,
not in choice of label, which is why $\kappa$ falls as far as it does once
blanks count.

\paragraph{The subset is not a random sample.} Tracing each item to its source
sheet shows the 500 are the alphabetical head of each category: all 9
prepositions, all 10 pronouns and all 6 interjections, then 305 adjectives,
150 nouns and 20 adverbs from the tops of those sheets. The corpus is 54\% noun and 25\%
verb; this subset is 61\% adjective and \textbf{is drawn from no verb
entries at all}. The only part-of-speech cells reading \texttt{Verb} anywhere
in the records are seven of Annotator 1's corrections to noun headwords. The
coefficients therefore describe agreement on this subset and are not an
estimate for the corpus. Establishing one requires a stratified
re-annotation covering verbs, which we leave to future work
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).

\section{Dataset derivation}
\label{app:dataset}

This appendix records the row-level derivation summarised in
Section~\ref{subsec:source}. Every count is reproducible from the released
code: \texttt{build\_merged\_eval\_set.py --dry-run --verify} rebuilds the
gold files from the source CSV and compares them against what is released,
and \texttt{build\_sense\_split\_eval\_set.py --verify} does the same for the
5,701-item set.

\paragraph{Source and provenance.} The digitised file
\texttt{Combined\_POS\_Lexicon.csv} holds 7,234 rows: 3,587
English$\rightarrow$Tigrinya entries followed by 3,647
Tigrinya$\rightarrow$English entries. It is a flattening of the working
annotation workbook, whose eleven part-of-speech sheets hold the same 7,234
rows with the same per-category counts (3,929 noun, 1,784 verb, 993
adjective, 194 adverb, and so on). The flattening lifts the inline
gender and number markers that the workbook keeps inside the Tigrinya field \texttt{(nf)} \ti{ወካሲት}, \ti{ከሳሲት} out into the label column, which
is the origin of the finer categories \texttt{(nm) noun masculine},
\texttt{(nf) noun feminine} and \texttt{(npl) noun plural}, which 107 export
rows carry. Compared as multisets of (English, Tigrinya) pairs, the two
files differ on 247 rows; stripping the parenthesised markers from both
reconciles all but 12 of those, so the markers account for the difference
without the 247 being the marked rows themselves. Twenty-four rows of the
gold files still contain an inline marker, including translation references
such as \texttt{(af)} \ti{ኣደናቒት}: these are residual and should be stripped,
and we note them rather than correct them silently so that the released gold
matches the outputs the models were scored on. The direction swap is
present in the workbook as well, in 3,635 of its 7,234 rows (50.2\%), so it
originates in digitisation rather than in the export.

\paragraph{7,234 counts rows, not independent annotations.} Of the 3,646
unique reverse-direction pairs, 1,448 (39.7\%) also occur in the forward
block.

\paragraph{From 7,234 to 5,783.} Merging the two directions deduplicates on
the (English, Tigrinya, features) triple rather than on the pair alone, so
that distinct senses of one form survive: \emph{estimate} $\rightarrow$
\ti{ግምት} is kept as both noun and verb, and \emph{grocer} $\rightarrow$
\ti{በዓል ድኳን} as both \texttt{noun} and \texttt{noun, masculine}. Expanding
comma-separated senses gives 5,864 atomic pairs.

\paragraph{From 5,783 to 5,775.} Each task script applies its own pair-level
deduplication when loading the gold, dropping 7 duplicate (English, Tigrinya)
pairs and 1 row with no English side.

\paragraph{From 5,775 to 5,701.} The two tagging tasks were run against a
gold file in which each multi-sense entry is represented by its first sense:
(\emph{a little}, \ti{ንእሽቶይ}, \ti{ቁሩብ}, \ti{ውሕድ}) was evaluated as
(\emph{a little}, \ti{ንእሽቶይ}). That intermediate file was not originally
released, so the task scripts as published would evaluate 5,775 items rather
than the 5,701 behind Sections~\ref{subsec:pos} and~\ref{subsec:morph}. We
reconstruct it from the released gold and verify the result row-for-row
against the saved model outputs: 5,701 items, no row missing, none extra, and
no label mismatch. The reconstructed file is released alongside the gold.
